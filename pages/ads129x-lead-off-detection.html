Lead-Off Detection in TI's ADS129x Family

A register-level walkthrough of how the ADS1298/ADS1299 biopotential front-ends detect a disconnected electrode — the DC current-injection method, the AC excitation method, the exact bit fields that configure each, and where the technique genuinely can't tell you what you want to know. Written for a hardware engineer who has never touched an ECG system before.

2026-07-07

↓ start reading

01 Why this exists

It isn't really "lead-off" detection

TI's own datasheet for this family opens with a clarification worth keeping in mind: [1]

"Although called lead-off detection, this feature is in fact electrode-off detection."

Think of the electrode-to-skin interface as a lossy, drifting resistor in series with your signal chain — not a wire. A gelled Ag/AgCl electrode starts out at maybe a few kΩ of contact impedance. Over hours, the conductive gel dries, the mechanical bond degrades, and that impedance climbs. TI's own measured data on a subject over five hours showed a wet electrode's impedance drifting by more than 10 kΩ as the gel dried out, while a dry, unprepped electrode started worse and stayed noisy [1]. At some point that "resistor" effectively becomes an open circuit — and your ADC has no built-in way to tell "very high impedance electrode" from "actual dangling wire" unless you deliberately design a circuit to probe it.

That's the whole job of the lead-off block inside the ADS1298 / ADS1299 (and siblings: ADS1294, ADS1296, ADS1298R, ADS1299-4, ADS1299-6): inject a small, known excitation signal into each input channel and watch how the front-end responds. Two channels of a differential ECG lead — plus, in most real systems, a right-leg-drive (RLD) reference electrode that biases the patient to a defined DC operating point — all need this check [1].

The core idea excitation in, response out

A known current or voltage is forced onto each input. As the patient-electrode path degrades toward an open circuit, the excitation signal dominates the input more and more — that's the signal a comparator or the ADC itself is watching for.

02 Method 1

DC lead-off: a current (or a resistor) pulling toward the rails

The simplest and lowest-overhead approach. The ADS129x can excite each input in one of two ways, selected by the VLEAD_OFF_EN bit in the LOFF register [2]:

If the electrode is properly connected, the excitation is small enough to barely perturb the real ECG signal. As the patient-electrode impedance rises, the excitation current develops a larger and larger voltage across it (Ohm's law, nothing exotic), and that voltage pulls the input rail-ward. When the electrode is fully open, the pull-up/pull-down (or current source) has nothing to fight and drives the input straight to the supply rail [1]. A comparator watches for exactly that condition.

DC lead-off — current-source variant simplified, one channel

Redrawn conceptually from TI's DC lead-off current-path diagram [1]. A closed current loop through the patient is required — TI's app note stresses that "failure to properly close the circuit loop can result in a false detection of lead status because the subject and monitoring system do not share the same grounding path."

The threshold DAC, exactly as TI specifies it

The comparator threshold isn't a single fixed voltage — it's programmable as a percentage of the supply rail via COMP_TH[2:0], and the two sides of the differential pair get mirrored thresholds around mid-supply [2]:

DATASHEET QUIRK

TI's own ADS1298 datasheet text describes this as "a comparator and a 4-bit DAC with levels that are set by the COMP_TH[2:0] bits" [2] — but COMP_TH[2:0] is a 3-bit field (8 codes), matching the 8-row table above exactly. This looks like leftover wording from an earlier revision rather than an actual 4th bit; we verified this directly against the current register map and it's genuinely a 3-bit field. Worth knowing if you're reading the datasheet prose literally.

The cost: offset error and noise

DC lead-off isn't free. Both excitation methods inject a small DC current through your signal-path impedance, and that shows up as an added input-referred offset. TI's app note gives the exact math and a worked example using their own eval board [1]:

WORKED EXAMPLE — FROM SBAA196A

Board input impedance Rin = 108.2 kΩ (32.1 kΩ series protection per channel + ~44 kΩ simulator/cable), 3 V supply:

V_offset = [(AVDD−AVSS) / (2·R_p + R_in)] · R_in with 10 MΩ pull-up/pull-down resistors → 16.14 mV of added offset.

V_offset = i_leadoff · R_in with a 6 nA current source → 650.4 µV of added offset — about 25× smaller.

This is exactly why TI recommends the smallest current-source setting (6 nA) rather than resistor pull-up/pull-down when offset matters, and it's directly measurable: TI's bench data shows the ECG baseline shifting by roughly this amount when DC lead-off is enabled, while the ECG waveform shape itself stays intact [1].

Noise tells a similar story: resistor-based lead-off adds Johnson-Nyquist thermal noise (4kTZin/Rp, standard resistor noise, nothing IC-specific); current-source lead-off is dominated instead by the current source's own flicker (1/f) noise, which scales with the magnitude of the current — one more reason the smallest available current (6 nA) is the usual default [1].

03 Method 2

AC lead-off: an out-of-band tone riding on the ECG signal

DC lead-off has a blind spot: it only sees resistance. Cable and electrode capacitance look like an open circuit to a DC excitation, so a purely capacitive fault path — increasingly common with dry-contact or non-contact capacitive electrodes — can slip past it [1][3]. AC lead-off fixes that by injecting a square-wave excitation and reading the amplitude of the response, which is sensitive to the total impedance magnitude (resistive and capacitive) at that frequency [3].

On the ADS1298, the excitation is generated the same way as DC (pull-up/pull-down structure at the input) but toggled at a fixed relationship to the ADC's own output data rate: fDR / 4, selected by FLEAD_OFF[1:0] = 01 [2]. Because that tone sits above the ECG band of interest, it passes through an on-chip antialiasing filter and shows up as an out-of-band component in the digitized output — separable from the real ECG signal with a digital bandpass/notch filter, so lead-off monitoring runs concurrently with normal signal acquisition rather than interrupting it [1][2].

Excitation amplitude vs. electrode impedance shape reproduced from TI SBAA196A Fig. 9 — illustrative, not exact data points

TI's own bench sweep (10 Ω to fully open, resistive load standing in for a degrading electrode) shows the AC excitation amplitude rising steeply as impedance approaches open circuit — from near-zero at low impedance to tens of millivolts peak-to-peak at open [1]. This monotonic relationship is what makes AC lead-off usable for graded "connection strength" tracking, not just a binary on/off flag.

Because the amplitude response is graded rather than a hard threshold trip, systems with enough digital processing headroom can track connectivity strength continuously, not just flag a binary disconnect [1]. Some designs go further and use in-phase/quadrature (I/Q) demodulation on the response to separate the resistive and capacitive parts of the electrode impedance instead of just reading a scalar magnitude [3] — useful if you specifically care about, say, gel drying (mostly resistive change) versus a lifting electrode (which changes the capacitive coupling too).

WARNING — FREQUENCY PLANNING IS NOT OPTIONAL

TI's application note is explicit about the one way to break AC lead-off entirely: "Careful attention is necessary to ensure that the frequency of the ac excitation signal does not overlap with either the high-frequency ECG or pacemaker signal components; otherwise, digital filtering cannot be used to isolate the ECG signal from the excitation signal." [1] Pacemaker pulses in particular have fast edges with real high-frequency content — pick an fDR/4 (or fixed-frequency, on the ADS1299) setting that lands clear of both, or your bandpass separation quietly stops working and you won't necessarily notice.

KEY FINDING

LOFF_STATP and LOFF_STATN aren't something you have to separately poll — they're transmitted as part of the 24-bit status word at the front of every conversion readout [2]. That means lead-off status is available on every single ADC sample, for free, with no extra SPI transaction and no arbitrary polling interval to choose.

Turning on DC lead-off — TI's own example

Straight from the ADS1298 datasheet's application-information section [2]:

WREG LOFF 0x13 // comparator threshold 95%/5%, pull-up/pull-down resistor DC lead-off
WREG CONFIG4 0x02 // turn on the DC lead-off comparators (PD_LOFF_COMP)
WREG LOFF_SENSP 0xFF // enable P-side lead-off sensing on all 8 channels
WREG LOFF_SENSN 0xFF // enable N-side lead-off sensing on all 8 channels

05 The reference electrode

RLD lead-off is a separate, gated mechanism

The right-leg-drive (RLD) electrode biases the patient to a defined common-mode operating point and drives the right-leg-drive amplifier's negative-feedback loop — it isn't a signal-measuring channel like IN1–IN8, so it can't be checked the same way [1][2]. The ADS129x handles it with a dedicated procedure: power down the RLD amplifier, then apply the same pull-up/pull-down-resistor-or-current-source-plus-comparator trick used for the main channels, using the same COMP_TH[7:5] threshold bits, and read the result back in a single dedicated status bit — RLD_STAT in the CONFIG3 register (0 = RLD connected, 1 = RLD not connected) [2].

WARNING — RLD LEAD-OFF ONLY WORKS WHILE RLD IS OFF

Quoted directly from the datasheet: "The current source, or pullup or pulldown resistor method has no function when the RLD amplifier is powered on." [2] In other words, you cannot continuously monitor RLD electrode connection while the RLD feedback loop is actively driving the patient — you have to momentarily disable RLD, check it, and re-enable it. Design your check-interval and your common-mode settling budget around that.

06 Family differences

The ADS1299's current-source range is genuinely striking: its top setting (24 µA) is 1,000× larger than the ADS1298's top setting (24 nA) — appropriate for EEG systems that may need to punch through much higher scalp-electrode impedances, but a setting you'd never want to leave at its default-adjacent high end on an ECG design without checking the offset-error math from Section 02 first.

07 Limitations

What lead-off detection genuinely can't tell you

INHERENT LIMITATION

Both methods are threshold-based: a comparator (DC) or an amplitude reading (AC) trips when impedance crosses a programmed line. That line cannot distinguish "electrode is completely detached" from "electrode is still touching skin but its impedance has drifted above your threshold" — a poorly-gelled or aging electrode reads identically to a disconnected one once it crosses the line [1]. This isn't a defect specific to TI's implementation; it's a fundamental property of any single-threshold impedance check. If you need to distinguish "bad contact" from "no contact," you need the AC method's graded amplitude output (Section 03) rather than the DC method's binary flag, and even then you're inferring, not measuring, contact quality directly.

Other constraints worth carrying into a design review:

  • DC lead-off only sees resistance. Electrode and cable capacitance present as an open circuit to a DC excitation, so a fault that's mostly capacitive (common with dry or non-contact capacitive electrodes) can be invisible to the DC method specifically [1][3].
  • DC lead-off always costs you offset and noise — see the worked 16.14 mV vs 650.4 µV example in Section 02. There's no "free" DC lead-off setting; you're always trading detection margin against signal integrity.
  • AC lead-off needs a closed loop for its return path, same as DC — TI's app note explicitly warns that an improperly closed current-return path (missing shared ground reference between patient and system) produces a false lead-off reading [1].
  • AC lead-off frequency choice is a hard constraint, not a tuning knob you can ignore — see the pacemaker/harmonic warning in Section 03.
  • RLD lead-off is not continuous — it only functions while the RLD amplifier is powered down (Section 05).
