BIOPOTENTIAL · ECG ACQUISITION

ECG via nRF + Electrode

A surface ECG is a 1 mV differential signal sitting on a ±300 mV DC pedestal, buried under common-mode mains interference 100–1000× its amplitude. The nRF52 SAADC can digitize it — but only after five distinct analog conditioning stages remove the offset, reject interference, and protect the hardware.

01 The ECG signal

What you are actually measuring

A surface ECG electrode picks up the cumulative electrical activity of the heart — a differential voltage between two points on the skin. Think of it as a very weak, band-limited signal generator in series with two large, noisy impedances (the skin contacts) and a common-mode interference source (mains hum coupled into the body).

1 mV

0.05–100 Hz

±300 mV

500 SPS

The DC offset problem

Each electrode develops a half-cell potential — a DC voltage generated by electrochemistry at the interface between the electrode metal and the conductive gel (think of it as a small battery built into the electrode-gel contact). For Ag/AgCl in standard conditions this voltage is approximately +0.223 V relative to a hydrogen reference electrode [5]. Two electrodes at slightly different temperatures or gel concentrations produce an offset of up to ±300 mV. This is not signal — it is a DC artifact 300× larger than the ECG itself. Any amplifier stage must remove it before applying gain, or the DC component will saturate everything downstream.

The electrode impedance problem

A wet Ag/AgCl electrode presents approximately 5 kΩ at 10 Hz with properly abraded skin [6]. A dry electrode is 50–1000× higher: 250 kΩ to 10 MΩ [7][8]. Any amplifier input connected to these electrodes must have an input impedance at least 100× the electrode impedance — otherwise the electrode-amp voltage divider attenuates the signal. For dry electrodes, you need input impedance in the hundreds of megaohms to gigaohm range.

CMRR MATH

The body acts as an antenna. Mains hum (50 or 60 Hz) couples capacitively into the body, producing a common-mode voltage of 1–10 V across both electrode inputs. This common-mode signal is 1000–10,000× larger than the 1 mV ECG [9]. If the amplifier has 60 dB CMRR (1000:1 rejection), a 1 V common-mode signal produces 1 mV of differential error — equal to the ECG itself. You need ≥90 dB CMRR to keep the mains residual well below the signal floor [10].

02 Why the nRF SAADC can't read ECG directly

Four blocking problems

The nRF52840 SAADC is a competent general-purpose ADC: 12-bit, up to 200 kSPS, 0.6 V internal reference, gain range 1/6–4 [11][12]. It is entirely unsuitable for direct electrode connection. Here is why:

Problem 1
0 CMRR
The SAADC is single-ended. Its negative input is shorted to ground internally [13]. It has no differential mode and therefore zero common-mode rejection. The 1–10 V mains hum passes straight through.
Problem 2
Saturation
The ±300 mV electrode DC offset added to the 1 mV signal has nowhere to go — the ADC input must stay within 0 to VDD [14]. Without AC coupling or active offset cancellation, the offset alone drives the ADC rail-to-rail.
Problem 3
~72 dB SNR
ECG requires approximately 80 dB SNR [15]. A 12-bit ADC gives 72 dB theoretical maximum. You need 14-bit effective resolution via hardware oversampling to close the gap — and that requires a clean, low-noise input signal first.
Problem 4
<5 MΩ
The SAADC input impedance is >5 MΩ idle, but drops during conversion when a sample-and-hold capacitor is switched in [16]. Exact value is not specified in the datasheet. Dry electrodes at 10 MΩ would form a resistive divider with the ADC — attenuating the signal.
WARNING

Direct electrode-to-ADC connection also bypasses all protection. The IEC 60601-1 defibrillation test fires 4724 V with a 9 µs rise time into the patient leads [17]. Without series protection resistors and a shunt clamp, the ADC input is destroyed instantly. Even without a defibrillator, ESD events from a patient touch are lethal to unprotected MCU pins.

Stage 1 — Electrode protection (defibrillation-proof path)

Place a 50 kΩ resistor in series with each electrode lead before any active circuitry. These resistors limit the peak current during a defibrillation event. In parallel with the downstream circuitry, add a shunt device — a gas-discharge tube or transient voltage suppressor (TVS) — to clamp the voltage before it reaches the INA inputs. IEC 60601-1 (Third Edition) requires that patient-connected devices absorb less than 10% of the defibrillation pulse energy [17]. The defibrillation test pulse peaks at 4724 V with a 9 µs rise time — conventional ESD diodes (with ns-scale turn-on) are marginal; a gas-discharge tube is preferred for the shunt [17].

The 50 kΩ series resistors also protect against patient ESD. They contribute noise (Johnson noise = √(4kTRΔf)), but at 50 kΩ and the ECG bandwidth (100 Hz), this is √(4 × 1.38×10⁻²³ × 310 × 50×10³ × 100) ≈ 290 nVrms — well below the 20 µVp-p noise floor of the AD8232 INA.

Stage 2 — Instrumentation amplifier

The INA converts the differential electrode voltage into a single-ended signal the ADC can read, while rejecting the large common-mode interference. Key requirements: input impedance far above the electrode impedance (≥100× rule — for 5 kΩ wet electrodes you need >500 kΩ; for 10 MΩ dry electrodes you need >100 MΩ), CMRR ≥80 dB, and gain high enough to lift the 1 mV ECG signal to a voltage the ADC can resolve cleanly.

The AD8232 provides 10 GΩ || 7.5 pF differential input impedance [18] — adequate for wet electrodes, marginal for dry. Its fixed INA gain is 100 V/V [20], and the total board gain (INA × output op-amp) is configurable — the evaluation board defaults to 1100 V/V.

Stage 3 — High-pass filter (0.05 Hz corner)

After the INA, the signal still contains the amplified DC offset (100 V/V × 300 mV = 30 V — which would saturate the op-amp stage). A high-pass filter (HPF) removes DC and low-frequency baseline wander before the output gain stage. The ECG standard requires 0.05 Hz corner frequency to pass the full clinical waveform. The AD8232 evaluation board ships with an HPF at 7 Hz (suitable only for heart rate detection, not diagnostic ECG) [23] — you must change the RC values if you need 0.05 Hz. For fc = 0.05 Hz with R = 10 MΩ, C = 1/(2π × 0.05 × 10×10⁶) ≈ 318 nF.

Stage 4 — Right-leg drive (RLD)

The RLD amplifier samples the common-mode voltage at the INA inputs, inverts it, amplifies it, and injects it back into the patient via a third electrode (typically on the right leg, hence the name). This creates a negative feedback loop that actively cancels the common-mode interference at the body, boosting the effective CMRR of the entire system by 20–40 dB beyond the INA alone. Think of it like noise-canceling headphones: instead of canceling sound in your ear, the RLD broadcasts an inverted copy of the mains hum back into the patient via the right leg electrode — the body's own electrical summing then cancels the interference at the electrode inputs.

WARNING — RLD oscillation

An uncompensated RLD circuit is inherently unstable. Without an external compensation network, the loop gain crosses unity with a rate-of-closure greater than 20 dB/dec — a guaranteed oscillation condition [21]. The AD8232 RLD uses an internal 150 kΩ resistor and requires an external 1 nF compensation capacitor to stabilize the loop; crossover is approximately 1 kHz, loop gain approximately 20 at 50/60 Hz [24]. Even with compensation, RLD stability depends on the cable impedance and must be validated per-design [25].

Stage 5 — Low-pass filter and optional notch

Before the ADC, a low-pass filter (LPF) with a corner at 150–200 Hz prevents aliasing. With a 500 SPS ADC rate, Nyquist is 250 Hz — a first-order RC at 150 Hz provides adequate roll-off. The AD8232 evaluation board defaults to approximately 25 Hz LPF, which is fine for pulse detection but too narrow for full diagnostic ECG (you lose ST-segment detail above 25 Hz).

A 50/60 Hz notch filter is optional but commonly added. Be aware: IIR notch filters cause 0–40 µV ringing artifacts in the ST-T segment, and there is a fundamental trade-off — narrower stopband reduces ECG distortion but worsens transient ringing [22]. For basic monitoring, skip the notch and rely on RLD + CMRR. For recording, apply the notch only in firmware as an FIR with linear phase.

04 The AD8232 — one IC, most of the signal chain

What it contains and what it does not

The AD8232 is a single-supply ECG front-end IC that integrates the INA, two HPF stages, a low-pass output op-amp, and the RLD amplifier into one package. For a wearable single-lead ECG it is the standard building block.

CONFLICT — CMRR: 80 dB vs. 90 dB requirement

Researcher A found ECG amplifiers require ≥90 dB CMRR to adequately reject mains interference [10]. Researcher B confirmed the AD8232 provides 80 dB CMRR [19]. These are not reconciled by the sources. In practice, the 80 dB AD8232 CMRR is supplemented by the RLD circuit and shielded cables — combined effective CMRR is significantly higher. For a research or wearable non-clinical device the AD8232 is widely used successfully. For a certified clinical device, the 10 dB shortfall is a design risk that must be addressed by risk analysis or a different front-end IC.

What the AD8232 does NOT provide

  • Defibrillation protection — no series resistors, no shunt clamp. You must add these externally.
  • Galvanic isolation — required for Type BF/CF classification under IEC 60601-1. The AD8232 shares a common ground with the nRF supply. Add an isolation barrier (ISO7741, ADuM1201, or similar) if you need regulatory compliance.
  • Lead-off detection beyond LOD± pins — the AD8232 provides lead-off detect pins but the detection current is small and not suitable for all electrode types.

05 Connecting the AFE output to the nRF SAADC

ADC configuration and voltage translation

The AD8232 output is a single-ended, rail-to-rail voltage centered at approximately half the supply. On a 3.3 V supply, the output idles at ~1.65 V (mid-rail) and swings ±300 mV for a typical ECG — staying comfortably within 0 to VDD that the SAADC requires [14][27].

SAADC CONFIGURATION FOR ECG

Reference: Internal 0.6 V. Gain: 1/6 → full-scale = 0.6 / (1/6) = 3.6 V (covers the full 0–3.3 V output swing) [28].
Resolution: 14-bit (via oversampling, 12-bit ADC × 4) to approach the 80 dB SNR requirement [29].
Acquisition time: If driving SAADC from AD8232 output (low-impedance <1 kΩ source), use minimum acquisition time (3 µs). The SAADC acquisition time must be set to allow the internal capacitor to fully settle [30].
Sampling rate: 500 SPS minimum for diagnostic ECG. At 500 SPS with 14-bit oversampling (4 samples averaged), the actual SAADC conversion rate is 2000 SPS — well within the BLE-limited 40 kSPS ceiling [31].

Conversion formula

Raw SAADC result to voltage [32]:

V = RESULT × 0.6 / (1/6) / 214 = RESULT × 3.6 / 16384

With gain 1/6, internal 0.6 V reference, 14-bit resolution: 1 LSB = 219.7 µV. After AD8232 gain of 1100×, 1 LSB referred to the electrode ≈ 0.2 µV — sufficient to resolve sub-millivolt ECG features.

Decoupling and source impedance

Nordic recommends 10 pF decoupling on the SAADC input pin, with an AC coupling capacitor close to the chip and source impedance matched to 50 Ω on the PCB trace [33]. Place the AD8232 output resistor (if used for LPF) as close as possible to the nRF pin to minimize the unshielded trace length picking up noise between the two ICs.

06 IEC 60601 safety classification

Isolation, leakage, and when you legally need what

IEC 60601-1 classifies patient-connected equipment by how much leakage current can flow through the patient connection. This determines whether galvanic isolation is required and at what voltage.

TypeLeakage (normal)Leakage (single fault)Isolation (output-to-ground)When required
B 100 µA 500 µA 500 VAC min External surface contact, low risk
BF 100 µA 500 µA 1500 VAC min Body surface contact requiring isolation [34]
CF 10 µA 50 µA 1500 VAC min Cardiac contact risk (any ECG near chest) [34]

Currents as low as 10–100 µA applied directly to the myocardium can trigger ventricular fibrillation [35]. This is why Type CF limits are 10 µA — the safety margin exists because ECG electrodes are placed on the chest near the heart, and a fault current path (through the electrode gel and skin into the myocardium) is plausible.

KEY FINDING — When isolation is required

CF classification is not automatically mandatory for all ECG equipment. The determination depends on intended use, patient connection type, and risk analysis [36]. For a research prototype or personal-use wearable powered by battery (no mains connection), the leakage current is often within Type B limits without isolation. For a mains-powered device or any device where mains voltage could appear on the patient connection under single-fault conditions, full galvanic isolation is required. Run the risk analysis — do not assume Type B is sufficient.

Adding galvanic isolation

If you need Type BF or CF compliance, place an isolation barrier between the electrode-side circuitry (AD8232 + analog power) and the nRF52 (digital side). Options:

  • Digital isolator — digitize the ECG on the patient side with a dedicated ADC (ADS1292, MAX30003), then send the digital output across a digital isolator (ISO7741, ADuM1201). Data crosses the barrier as SPI/I2C; no analog crossing needed.
  • Isolated power — run the patient-side circuitry from an isolated DC/DC converter (e.g., R05CT05S or similar). The analog signal can then cross as a modulated signal (capacitively or optically coupled).

Input-to-output isolation must withstand 4000 VAC minimum; output-to-ground for Type BF/CF must withstand 1500 VAC [34].

07 PCB layout and practical tips

Where ECG designs die on the board

Separate analog and digital power planes

Running the AD8232 from the same power rail as the nRF52 radio is a common mistake. Power planes here means separate voltage regulators and ground areas on the PCB — one regulator and one ground pour for the ECG analog front-end, a separate regulator and ground pour for the nRF52 and its radio. The BLE radio transmit burst injects switching noise directly into the analog supply, and this appears as periodic artifacts in the ECG trace. Use a separate LDO for the analog side, referenced to a clean analog ground [37]. A ferrite bead or RC filter between the digital and analog supply rails is the minimum acceptable approach.

BLE COUPLING WARNING

The nRF52 BLE radio transmits at 0 dBm to +8 dBm at 2.4 GHz in bursts of ~1.25 ms per connection event. This couples capacitively into every nearby trace. Your ECG signal is 1 mV — even −60 dBm of coupling (−60 dBm = 1 µW into 50 Ω = 7 mVp-p) is 7× the signal amplitude. Separate power supplies and physical separation of analog components from the antenna are non-negotiable [37].

Guard rings

Surround the AD8232 and its passive components with a driven guard ring connected to the mid-supply reference voltage (the same voltage that drives the INA reference input). A guard ring driven to the same potential as the signal common mode eliminates leakage currents from the PCB surface resistance that would otherwise inject noise into the high-impedance input nodes [38]. The guard trace should be continuous with no gaps or branches, and as wide as space allows.

Cable and electrode routing

Power-line interference couples capacitively into both the electrode leads and the body. Shielded cables reduce coupling into the leads, but the electrode tip itself is unshielded — the high-impedance tip remains a capacitive antenna at the mains frequency [39]. Short cables reduce capacitance. Twisted pairs reduce differential interference pickup. The RLD electrode handles what remains.

Component placement checklist

  • AD8232 supply decoupling: 0.1 µF ceramic immediately at the supply pin, 1 µF bulk within 5 mm [40]
  • RLD capacitor (1 nF): within 2 mm of the AD8232 RLD pin — long traces add parasitic inductance that destabilizes the loop
  • HPF capacitors: use low-leakage film capacitors for the 0.05 Hz path — ceramic X5R/X7R dielectrics have DC bias dependence that shifts the corner frequency
  • nRF52 SAADC input: 10 pF to analog ground at the pin [33]
  • Electrode protection resistors (50 kΩ): physically at the board edge, before any other circuitry — they protect everything downstream

08 Complete build summary

What you need, in order

MINIMUM VIABLE ECG WITH nRF52

ICs: AD8232 (AFE) + nRF52832 or nRF52840 (MCU/BLE)
Electrodes: 3× Ag/AgCl — LA (left arm), RA (right arm), RL (right leg / RLD)
Protection: 2× 50 kΩ series resistors on LA and RA; 2× TVS shunt to analog ground
HPF: Change AD8232 HPF components for 0.05 Hz corner (R = 10 MΩ, C ≈ 320 nF) for diagnostic ECG, or leave at 7 Hz for pulse detection only
RLD compensation: 1 nF capacitor on AD8232 RLDREF pin (already specified in datasheet)
ADC config: Gain 1/6, internal 0.6 V ref, 14-bit (oversampled), 500 SPS
Power: Separate LDO for AD8232 analog supply; ferrite bead separation from nRF52 digital supply
Isolation: Not required for battery-powered research prototype; required for mains-connected or certified medical device

The nRF52832 is already proven in commercial ECG wearables — the AppSens ECG247 Smart Heart Sensor uses it for cardiac rhythm disorder detection [41]. The path from electrode to BLE is well-trodden; the work is in the analog signal chain, not the digital side.
