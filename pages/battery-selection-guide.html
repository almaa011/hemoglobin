POWER · BATTERY SELECTION

Choosing a Rechargeable Battery for a Wearable/Biomedical Device

A framework for picking the right cell chemistry, capacity, and charge circuit for a wrist-worn, patch, or handheld biomedical device — plus the battery-basics math (C-rate, cycle life, internal resistance) you need to read a cell datasheet correctly. Rechargeable chemistries only: Li-ion, LiPo, LiFePO4, NiMH.

2026-07-11

↓ start reading
TL;DR

For most wrist-worn or patch biomedical electronics, a small Li-ion/LiPo pouch cell (3.6–3.7V nominal, 4.2V full charge) is the default choice — it has the best energy density of the common rechargeable chemistries (up to ~250 Wh/kg) [8], and the ecosystem of fuel-gauge ICs, linear chargers, and protection circuitry around it is mature and cheap.

Reach for LiFePO4 only when safety margin matters more than size — it trades ~30–40% energy density [8] for a thermal-runaway onset roughly 60°C higher than standard Li-ion (~270°C vs ~210°C) [24] and 2–3x the cycle life at a given charge voltage [7]. NiMH is rarely the right call for new designs today — lower energy density (60–120 Wh/kg) and higher self-discharge (1–5%/day) than Li-ion, though it remains relevant for AA/AAA-compatible or 1.2V-per-cell legacy designs [9].

The single biggest lever you control is charge voltage. Charging to 4.20V/cell gives the most capacity but only 300–500 cycles; dropping to 4.10V/cell roughly doubles cycle life to 600–1,000 cycles at the cost of some runtime [2] — most fuel-gauge/charger ICs let you set this in firmware.

For skin-contact wearables specifically, thermal budget is a bigger practical constraint than most datasheets suggest: the commonly cited "45°C device-safe" limit is derived from Li-ion's safe operating range, not human skin tolerance — actual burn threshold for 8-hour continuous contact is only 43°C [25].

If the device ships anywhere by air, note that as of January 1, 2026 all lithium batteries — even pre-installed in the device — are increasingly constrained to ≤30% state of charge (SOC — the battery-industry shorthand for "percent full") for air transport, a rule that now also applies to batteries packaged with devices [32].

01 Battery basics

Chemistry is the first decision — everything else follows from it

Four rechargeable chemistries cover nearly all wearable/biomedical designs: Li-ion (LCO/NMC), LiPo, LiFePO4, and NiMH. Li-ion and LiPo share almost identical electrochemistry and voltage behavior — LiPo just uses a polymer/gel electrolyte in a flexible pouch instead of a rigid can, which is why it dominates thin wearable form factors.

Energy density by chemistry

Standard Li-ion/NMC cells top out around 150–220 Wh/kg, with the ceiling for commercial Li-ion cells generally cited near 250 Wh/kg [8]. LiFePO4 trades roughly a third of that density (90–160 Wh/kg) for safety margin [8]. NiMH trails both at 60–120 Wh/kg [9].

Rule of thumb

Every 0.10V you shave off the peak charge voltage roughly doubles cycle life, at the cost of proportionally less capacity per charge [2]. This is a firmware-level knob on most fuel-gauge/charger ICs (see §4) — it's often the cheapest "different battery" you can buy without changing hardware.

02 Battery basics

The specs that actually determine runtime and safety margin

Capacity (mAh) alone doesn't tell you runtime — you also need C-rate, internal resistance, and self-discharge. These interact, and datasheets often quote the most flattering number rather than the one that matches your actual use case.

C-rate: capacity depends on how fast you pull it out

1C means "discharge the full rated capacity in one hour." A 200mAh cell at 1C delivers 200mA for an hour; at 2C it would (nominally) deliver 400mA for 30 minutes [1].

Discharging faster than rated pulls usable capacity down — not just runtime, but total mAh delivered. At 2C, a cell typically yields only ~95% of its rated capacity; at 0.5C it can exceed 100% because internal losses that turn energy into heat scale with current [1]. Watch the current draw of your PPG (photoplethysmography — the optical pulse sensor's) LED drive or radio TX burst against this — a cell rated generously at a slow test rate can under-deliver in a bursty duty cycle.

Standard "Energy Cell" Li-ion is typically protection-limited to 1C discharge; "Power Cell" variants of the same chemistry are built for up to 10C [1] — relevant if your design has short, high-current bursts (e.g. a haptic motor or RF power amplifier).

Cycle life, depth of discharge, and what "end of life" means

One cycle = one full charge-discharge; "end of life" is industry-standard defined as 70–80% of original capacity remaining (State of Health) [3]. Depth of Discharge (DOD) is how much of the rated capacity you actually use per cycle — pulling 80 of 100Ah before recharging is 80% DOD [3].

Internal resistance directly costs you voltage headroom under load — a higher-resistance cell sags more under a current pulse, which matters if your AFE/analog front-end has a tight supply-voltage floor. If you're storing devices for long periods (retail shelf, clinical trial kit assembly), store at ~50% state of charge to minimize self-discharge losses, not full charge [6].

03 Selection framework

A decision order for picking the actual part

Work through these in order — each one eliminates options before you get to part numbers. Skipping ahead to "which mAh cell" before nailing down form factor and duty cycle is the most common way teams end up re-spinning a design.

Key finding

Steps 3 and 4 are the ones teams most often shortcut — and they're exactly the ones a peer-reviewed thermal study and an electronics-cooling trade publication flag as the least forgiving. A cell sized correctly for average current can still misbehave under peak current [1], and a device thermally "safe" by battery standards can still be too hot for 8-hour skin contact [25] (see §6).

04 Implementation

Charging, fuel gauging, and protection circuitry

A bare cell is not a battery pack — you need a fuel gauge, a charger IC, and protection circuitry around it before it's usable. This is where most of the actual BOM and firmware work lives.

Fuel gauge ICs

Fuel gauges track remaining charge so firmware can report a % battery figure and trigger low-battery behavior. TI's BQ27427 is a representative example: supports 100–8,000 mAh cells via TI's "Impedance Track" algorithm, I2C at 400 kHz, three selectable cell profiles (4.2V/4.35V/4.4V), a tiny 9-ball 1.62×1.58mm package with a 7mΩ integrated sense resistor, 50µA active / 9µA sleep current draw, rated −40 to 85°C [11].

Charger ICs

Charger ICs fall into two families: simple linear chargers and switch-mode/buck chargers with power-path management. TI's BQ25120 is a linear-charger-plus-buck-regulator example built for exactly this class of device: 5–300mA configurable charge current, 0.5%-accurate battery voltage regulation adjustable 3.6–4.65V in 10mV steps over I2C (this is your knob for the cycle-life-vs-capacity tradeoff from §1), a buck converter that stays efficient down to 10µA loads, 700nA quiescent current, and 5V USB input tolerant to 20V line transients [13].

Protection circuitry

A Protection Circuit Module (PCM) sits between the cell and the rest of the system, enforcing hard voltage/current limits independent of firmware. Typical wearable LiPo protection circuits implement overvoltage-charge protection, undervoltage-discharge protection, short-circuit protection, and overcurrent protection, going to an open-circuit state if any threshold is exceeded [14]. Treat this as a second, dumb, always-on layer beneath your smart fuel-gauge/charger IC — not a replacement for it.

Connectors

JST-PH (2.0mm pitch) is the de facto standard connector for small single-cell (1S) LiPo packs — 2A per contact / 100V rating (comfortably above the ~1C charge current of most small packs), AWG24-32 wire, 30mΩ max contact resistance, PA66 nylon UL94V-0 housing with tin-plated brass contacts, rated −25 to 85°C [13].

06 Failure modes

Where battery-selection guidance oversimplifies

Three places where the common shorthand doesn't survive contact with the primary literature. These are the findings our adversarial research pass specifically surfaced.

Thermal runaway isn't one failure mode — it's (at least) two paths with different signatures

A peer-reviewed thermal-abuse study on Li-ion cells found overcharge-triggered runaway and overheat-triggered runaway behave differently: overcharge above the 4.2V cutoff drives lithium dendrites through the separator (~800 seconds in) and peaks at 411°C cathode temperature; pure overheating past ~120°C peaks hotter, at 516°C [20].

Below full runaway, there's a cascade of quieter failures worth knowing: separator pore closure at ~80°C, SEI decomposition at ~120°C, electrolyte decomposition above ~130°C, cathode oxygen release above ~200°C [20] — each one degrades the cell further even if it never reaches ignition.

Thermal failure cascade
Li-ion cell, °C, peer-reviewed [20]

The two runaway peaks (411°C overcharge-path, 516°C overheat-path) are not the same event reaching different temperatures — they're two distinct failure chains that happen to share the same intermediate decomposition steps below ~200°C.

Nuance — "LiFePO4 doesn't overheat or explode"

This marketing-style claim, common in wearable-medtech blog content, overstates what the underlying chemistry actually buys you. Verified data shows LiFePO4's thermal-runaway onset is genuinely higher than NMC's (~270°C vs ~210°C) and it doesn't release oxygen during decomposition, which reduces fire-propagation risk [24] — but it is a higher safety margin, not immunity. Size the safety margin accordingly rather than treating LiFePO4 as fail-safe.

Conflict — cycle-life numbers depend heavily on cell format, and marketing figures often don't say which

One source reports real-world cycle life of 3,000–5,000 cycles at 20–80% DOD for LiFePO4, dropping to ~800 cycles under 2C fast charge, with degradation 3x faster at 45°C vs 25°C [21] — but that source's context (electric vehicles, energy storage stations) suggests large-format cells, not small wearable pouch cells. Battery University's cycle-life data for standard small Li-ion cells (300–500 cycles at 4.2V charge, §1) is an order of magnitude lower [2]. Don't apply EV/ESS-class cycle-life marketing numbers to a small wearable cell — ask the specific cell's manufacturer for cycle data at your actual charge voltage and DOD.

The state-of-charge window you cycle in matters more than "keep it in the middle"

A peer-reviewed study found cycling within a 65–85% SOC window produced more active-lithium loss than cycling in either 45–65% or 75–95% windows [22] — the opposite of the intuitive "avoid the extremes, stay in the middle" heuristic. The mechanism is mechanical: graphite particles undergo maximum surface dilation transitioning through that specific range.

LiPo swelling is a chemistry-compromise signal, not a cosmetic issue

Swelling comes from electrolyte decomposing into gas, commonly from overvoltage charging [23]. Even minor swelling doesn't always mean imminent failure, but it does indicate the internal chemistry is compromised — professional assessment or replacement is the safe response, not "keep using it until it gets worse" [23].

Conflict — "45°C is device-safe" vs. actual skin-burn thresholds

An electronics-cooling industry article states wearable device skin temperature is "generally accepted" to have a ~45°C maximum, derived from Li-ion's 0–45°C safe operating range [18]. A separate peer-reviewed-adjacent source on wearable/implantable thermal challenges gives actual human burn thresholds by contact duration: <48°C for >10-minute contact, but only 43°C for 8-hour continuous contact; comfort requires <39°C [25]. A device sitting at the "battery-safe" 45°C limit is already above the 8-hour burn threshold — battery safety and skin safety are not the same budget, and a continuously-worn device needs to design to the tighter one.

Warning — shipping rule changed in 2026

As of January 1, 2026, lithium batteries packaged together with a device (not just shipped standalone) must also be charged to ≤30% state of charge for air transport — previously this 30%-SOC rule applied mainly to standalone cells/batteries. Only batteries already installed and running inside their end device retain an exemption to ship at higher charge [32]. If your logistics chain ships partially-assembled units by air, check which category you fall into now — this is a live regulatory change, not background context.
