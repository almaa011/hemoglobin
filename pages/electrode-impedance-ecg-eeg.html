BIOPOTENTIAL SENSING · ELECTRODES

Electrode impedance,
explained for a newbie.

EEG and ECG both pick up tiny voltages from the body through metal electrodes stuck on skin. "Impedance" shows up twice in that chain, and confusingly, you want it low in one place and high in another. This page untangles which is which, why it matters, and what real numbers look like.

KEY FINDING

There are two impedances in this circuit, and they need opposite treatment. The electrode-to-skin contact is a messy, leaky junction you want as electrically "quiet" as possible — low impedance, ideally a few kΩ or less. The amplifier that reads the signal needs the opposite: input impedance so high (MΩ to GΩ) that it barely draws any current at all.

Think of it as a voltage divider: skin-electrode impedance is a resistor in series with your signal, amplifier input impedance is a resistor to ground. If the amplifier's resistor isn't vastly bigger than the electrode's, your signal gets divided away before you ever see it. Modern chips make the amplifier side so high-impedance (often >1 GΩ) that this loss is negligible even with dry, poorly-prepped electrodes — which is why consumer EEG/ECG devices with dry pads can work at all.

The catch: high electrode impedance doesn't just risk signal loss — it also makes the signal noisier and more sensitive to hum, motion, and imbalance between electrodes. So "low is good, and modern amps forgive high" is true, but not a free pass.

01 First, what "impedance" even means here

Impedance = resistance that also depends on frequency

Impedance is just resistance's more complicated cousin — resistance that changes with signal frequency, measured in the same unit (ohms, Ω). A plain resistor has the same resistance whether the signal is DC or wiggling at 1 kHz. The electrode-skin junction does not behave that way — it acts partly resistive and partly capacitive, so its opposition to current flow changes depending on how fast the signal is moving.

The electrode-skin interface, modeled as a circuit simplified Randles-type model

Three things happen where metal meets skin. A small DC battery-like voltage appears (the half-cell potential) from the metal reacting with the skin's moisture. A capacitor-like layer forms right at the surface (double-layer capacitance). And a resistive path exists both through that layer and through the tissue itself. All three combine into "electrode impedance."[1]

Where each piece comes from

  • Half-cell potential — every metal touching an electrolyte (your sweat/skin) develops a small, steady voltage, just like a tiny battery. For silver/silver-chloride (Ag/AgCl) — the standard clinical electrode metal — this is about 199 mV, and importantly it's stable and low compared to other metals.[2]
  • Double-layer capacitance — ions from your skin line up against the metal surface like a tiny capacitor. Typical value: 20–60 µF per cm² of electrode area.[1]
  • Charge-transfer & Warburg terms — resistive/diffusion effects that make the impedance drop as frequency rises (ions don't have to travel as far per cycle at high frequency).[1]
Why Ag/AgCl and not bare steel or gold?

Ag/AgCl is "nonpolarizable" — current can pass through the metal-skin junction via a real chemical reaction (Ag ⇌ AgCl + e⁻) instead of just charging up like a capacitor. That gives it a low, stable half-cell potential and low interface impedance, which is why it's the default for clinical ECG/EEG electrodes despite dry metal or gold pins being simpler to manufacture.[2]

02 The two impedances that matter

Source impedance (electrode) vs. load impedance (amplifier)

Your body's signal has to pass through the electrode impedance before it reaches the amplifier — and the amplifier's own input impedance decides how much of that signal survives the trip. This is a plain voltage divider, the same one you'd analyze for any two-resistor circuit.

Signal chain as a voltage divider Zelectrode in series, Zamp to reference

The body's signal source sees Zelectrode in series, then Zamp as the path to reference. Only the fraction Zamp / (Zamp + Zelectrode) of the original signal reaches the amplifier's input. Make Zamp huge relative to Zelectrode, and that fraction rounds up to ~100%.

General engineering knowledge — not from a specific source

This voltage-divider math is standard circuit theory, not a domain-specific research finding — no citation needed, just Ohm's law applied to two impedances in series.

This is the whole reason dry electrodes work at all. Even a electrode-skin impedance in the megohm range loses almost nothing once the amplifier's input impedance is in the gigaohm range or higher — the ratio, not the absolute electrode number, is what decides signal loss.

The second problem: mismatch breaks noise rejection

Biopotential amplifiers reject "common-mode" noise (mains hum, EMI) by subtracting two electrode inputs that should see identical interference.[3] If the two electrodes have different impedances, that interference no longer cancels — part of it turns into a fake differential signal the amplifier can't tell from real biosignal.[3]

WARNING

Matched-but-high beats mismatched-but-low. Two electrodes at 500 kΩ each are generally safer for noise rejection than one at 2 kΩ and one at 50 kΩ — the mismatch, not the absolute value, is what wrecks common-mode rejection.[3]

03 Real numbers

What impedance actually looks like in practice

Wet gelled Ag/AgCl electrodes sit in the low-kΩ range; dry electrodes can be 100–1000× higher. Standards exist for the wet/prepped case; dry electrodes are only usable because amplifier input impedance has gotten so extreme.

CONFLICT

"Acceptable" impedance isn't one number — sources disagree by up to 7×. The ratified ANSI/AAMI ECG standard is <2 kΩ.[4] A commercial electrode-prep guide cites a looser 15 kΩ ceiling as "normal."[13] EEG guidance separately allows up to 10 kΩ.[6] Ratified standards and peer-reviewed sources are given precedence on this page; the 15 kΩ figure is flagged as a secondary/commercial source, not a standard.

CONFLICT

Two studies of "dry electrode" impedance disagree by roughly 500×. One reports dry electrodes at ~5.15 kΩ·cm² (area-normalized);[7] another measured dry stainless-steel/titanium electrodes in vivo at 2.0–2.7 MΩ·cm² at 10 Hz.[8] Neither source specifies enough measurement conditions (contact pressure, exact frequency, surface finish) to reconcile the gap — flagged here rather than silently resolved.

It changes over time, too

Fresh gel first gets better, then gets worse. In the first ~15 minutes after application, impedance typically drops and stabilizes as the gel settles and hydrates the skin — one study measured a ~13% drop by minute 10, then only ~3% variation after minute 15.[14]

Hours later, the opposite trend takes over: as gel dries out, impedance climbs back up — one study measured a rise from ~5 kΩ to ~15 kΩ over 5 hours.[15] No single study tracked both phases together, so this two-phase story is this page's synthesis of two separate measurements, not one continuous curve.

04 What goes wrong

High electrode impedance isn't "free" even with a great amplifier

A high-impedance amplifier fixes signal attenuation, but it doesn't fix noise. Three separate failure modes get worse as electrode impedance rises, independent of the voltage-divider math.

Failure modeWhat happensMitigation
Noisier signalHigh electrode impedance directly increases susceptibility to mains hum (50/60 Hz) and general EMI pickup via capacitive coupling.[16]Active/buffered electrodes; shielding
Fewer usable trials (EEG research)Recovering a clean ERP signal at high impedance can require ~3× more averaged trials (35 vs. 12) to hit the same statistical power, and the effect worsens in warm/humid rooms.[17]Skin prep; lower ambient humidity
Motion artifactMovement physically disturbs the ion layer at the electrode-skin interface, changing impedance dynamically and injecting a spurious low-frequency voltage — not just wire wiggle.[18]Mechanical strain relief; adhesive electrodes; motion-reference channel
Common misconception, worth naming directly

"High impedance electrodes are bad" is often stated without saying which impedance. It's electrode-skin impedance that should be low; amplifier input impedance should be high. Conflating the two is one of the most common points of confusion in beginner explanations of this topic.

05 How engineers fix it

Buffer at the electrode, measure impedance in real time

The two production techniques that make dry, high-impedance electrodes practical: active (buffered) electrodes, and continuous impedance monitoring built into the AFE chip.

Active electrode: buffering right at the skin unity-gain op-amp at the contact point

A unity-gain buffer sits physically at the electrode. It presents a very high impedance to the skin (so the divider math above stays favorable) and a very low impedance — under 1 Ω — out to the cable.[19] Interference picked up by the cable now fights a near-zero-ohm source instead of a hundred-kilohm one, which is why active electrodes resist hum and motion far better than passive ones.[19]

One real design: a spring-loaded dry EEG electrode with an on-electrode buffer measured 1.131 µVRMS noise and a ~147.5% improvement in effective impedance across the 0.5–50 Hz EEG band compared to the unbuffered case.[20]

Lead-off / impedance-check circuits in real ICs

Modern AFE chips (e.g. TI's ADS129x family, AFE4960) build impedance measurement directly into the chip, so firmware can flag a bad or detached electrode before the user notices garbage data.[21]

  • DC lead-off detection — monitors small DC offset shifts on each electrode. Simple, low signal impact, but adds its own offset error.[21]
  • AC lead-off / impedance measurement — injects a small AC excitation current and measures the resulting voltage to compute real impedance continuously, even mid-recording. Needs more digital processing to separate from the real signal.[21][11]
KEY FINDING

If you're specifying a biopotential AFE for a new design: look for input impedance in the ≥1 GΩ range as a baseline (matches TI AFE4960 and similar parts[11]), and prefer a part with built-in AC impedance/lead-off measurement so you can catch bad contacts in firmware instead of guessing from noisy data.
