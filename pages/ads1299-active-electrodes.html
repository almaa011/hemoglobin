Active Electrodes for the ADS1299

An active electrode is nothing more than a unity-gain op-amp buffer sitting at the electrode, before the cable. This page shows the exact circuit, wires it into the ADS1299's inputs pin-by-pin, and explains why every part — the bias resistor, the driven guard, the low-bias-current op-amp — has to be there.

2026-07-14

↓ start reading
TL;DR

An active electrode = a unity-gain buffer op-amp mounted on the electrode. It converts a high, unstable source impedance (a dry contact can be ~10 MΩ [10]) into a low output impedance of a few ohms, so the cable run to the ADS1299 stops picking up mains hum and motion artifact.

Wire it as a non-inverting follower: electrode → op-amp + input, output → back to − input and down the cable to an ADS1299 INxP pin. Add a high-value DC bias resistor (MΩ–GΩ) from the + input to the reference — without it the op-amp's input bias current has no return path, charges the electrode, and the output rails. [13]

Pick an op-amp with very low input bias current (sub-pA to single-digit pA — OPA2376, OPA378, TLC272) so it doesn't create DC drift into the electrode. [4][7] Honest caveat: with wet Ag/AgCl electrodes and short cables the ADS1299 alone already rivals lab gear [8], and at least one bench test found active electrodes barely changed the recorded signal [11] — the payoff is real mainly for dry / high-impedance electrodes and long or moving cables.

01 The problem

Why a wire from a scalp electrode is a bad antenna

The electrode is a high-impedance source, and high-impedance sources make cables act like antennas.

Model the electrode–skin contact as a voltage source (your µV-level EEG) behind a series impedance. A wet Ag/AgCl contact is roughly 10 kΩ; a dry electrode is 100–1000× worse, around 10 MΩ. [10]

Now the cable from that node to the amplifier has capacitance to everything around it — mains wiring, your body, the air. That stray capacitance forms a divider and a current-injection path.

THE MECHANISM

Two things go wrong when source impedance is high and it sits behind a long cable:

  • Interference pickup — 50/60 Hz displacement current from mains couples into the high-impedance node; the higher the impedance, the bigger the resulting voltage.
  • Motion artifact — cable flexing changes its stray capacitance; that modulates the signal because the source can't "stiffly" drive the line.
  • CMRR collapse — the ADS1299's −110 dB CMRR [1] assumes matched source impedances. Two electrodes with different, drifting MΩ impedances unbalance the inputs and convert common-mode hum into differential error.

Plain version: a microvolt signal coming out of a 10 MΩ source down a wobbling cable is almost impossible to keep clean. You want to fix that at the electrode, before the wire.

02 The idea

Put a buffer on the electrode

An active electrode locally buffers the µV EEG signal right at the scalp, before any cabling. [18]

The fix is an impedance converter: a unity-gain op-amp follower physically mounted on the electrode. Its + input sees the huge source impedance; its output is a stiff, low-impedance copy of that same voltage — same volts, but now able to drive a cable.

Electrode ~10 MΩ → ×1 buffer → Zout ≈ few Ω → cable → ADS1299 INxP
KEY FINDING

The gain is ×1 — it does not amplify voltage. Its entire job is to transform impedance. The published review of wearable EEG active electrodes puts it plainly: "the low output impedance of an AE mitigates cable motion artifacts, thus enabling the use of high-impedance dry electrodes." [18] (General engineering knowledge: a follower's low Zout means stray cable capacitance is charged by the op-amp, not by your fragile electrode node.)

Some designs add a small gain or a high-pass, but the canonical active electrode — OpenEEG, OpenBCI, and most DIY builds — is a unity-gain follower with a driven guard. [6]

03 The circuit

The full schematic, part by part

Four things live on the electrode: the buffer, a DC bias resistor, a driven guard/shield, and power. Here is how they connect.

One active electrode → one ADS1299 channelnon-inverting unity-gain buffer

The buffer (teal) sits on the electrode. The DC bias resistor (amber) gives input bias current a path to the reference. The op-amp output does double duty: it feeds the cable to INxP and drives the cable guard/shield (blue, dashed) so the shield tracks the signal. Power for the op-amp is carried down the same cable from the board's ±2.5 V rails.

Component by component — and why it's there

The buffer
Non-inverting op-amp with output tied straight back to its − input → gain = ×1. Turns ~10 MΩ source impedance into a few ohms of output impedance. This is the whole point of the circuit.
Rbias
High-value resistor (megohms to gigohms) from the + input to the reference. Mandatory. An op-amp + input still sinks/sources a tiny bias current; the DC-blocking electrode interface can't supply it, so without Rbias that current charges the interface and the output drifts to the rail. [13] One published interface uses a 1 kΩ input R plus a 1 MΩ resistor for a ~10 MΩ input impedance. [16]
Driven guard
The op-amp output also drives the cable shield (and a guard ring around the input trace). Because the shield sits at the same voltage as the signal, the input-to-shield capacitance sees 0 V across it and effectively disappears — no charging current, so the high-impedance node stays fast and quiet. [6] (Classic guarding technique.)
Power
The op-amp needs supply rails at the electrode. OpenBCI runs a shielded 3-wire cable — signal, +2.5 V, −2.5 V — powering the buffer from the board rails, with the shield to GND. [6]
WHERE TO TAP POWER

OpenBCI's community build powers the buffers from AVDD/AVSS (±2.5 V) [6], but experienced builders warn against it: "any noise back-coupled from the active into AVDD/AVSS will show up on all channels" — they recommend powering from DVDD/GND instead. [7] The buffer's own supply current is a noise source; keep it off the analog rails your ADC references.

04 The one that bites you

Why the bias resistor is non-negotiable

Skip the DC bias path and your active electrode works for a minute, then saturates.

An op-amp input draws a small bias current, Ib. The electrode–skin interface is capacitive (it blocks DC). So that bias current has nowhere to go except into the interface capacitance, charging it up like a slow integrator.

dV/dt = Ib / Cinterface
// bias current with no DC return path → voltage ramps until the output rails

The classic study measured exactly this — drift scales directly with the op-amp's bias current: [12]

Op-ampInput bias currentBaseline drift @ 60 min
µA74120 nA28 %
LF3563 pA9 %
AD5230.25 pA2 %

Two defenses, used together:

  • Give the current a path — Rbias from the + input to a stable reference. Now Ib flows through Rbias instead of the electrode. The trade-off: Rbias sets the input impedance and adds thermal noise, so it wants to be as large as tolerable (MΩ–GΩ).
  • Pick a low-Ib op-amp — sub-pA to single-digit pA so there's barely any current to deal with in the first place (see the op-amp table below).
CONFLICT — surfaced, not resolved

Does an active electrode actually help an ADS1299 rig? The wearable-EEG review and several papers say yes — local buffering enables dry/high-impedance electrodes and cuts cable artifact. [18] The ADS1299's own 300 pA bias and ~1 GΩ DC input impedance are "small… may not be suitable for dry passive electrodes." [19] But one OpenBCI bench test found active electrodes dropped measured contact impedance from ~40 kΩ to ~4 kΩ yet left the recorded signal and 50 Hz noise "roughly the same… probably not enough to warrant the extra costs." [11] And the ADS1299 with wet Ag/AgCl electrodes already matches lab-grade systems below 40 Hz. [8]

Reading: the payoff is real for dry / high-impedance electrodes and long or moving cables. For wet electrodes on short leads, the ADS1299 alone is usually enough — both results can be true.

05 Into the AFE

Wiring the buffer output into the ADS1299

The buffer output is just a low-impedance voltage — it lands on a normal channel input pin; the ADS1299's shared reference and bias pins do the rest.

INxP / INxN
The buffered signal drives the positive input of a channel. The other input is either a second active electrode (differential pair) or the shared reference via SRB.
SRB1 / SRB2
Shared-reference-buffer pins routed to all eight channels' MUXes. [1] Tie SRB1 to your reference electrode to make every channel a measurement against that common reference — the usual "referential montage" EEG wiring.
BIAS (RLD)
The on-chip bias/right-leg-drive amplifier senses the common-mode from selected channels and injects an inverted copy back into a body electrode to cancel mains hum. [17] The EEG-FE board uses Rf = 392 kΩ, Cf = 10 nF, Rp = 4.99 kΩ in its RLD feedback. [3] A 1 MΩ series resistor on BIASOUT limits patient current. [9]
AVDD/AVSS · DVDD
Source of the ±2.5 V (analog) or digital rails you carry down the cable to power the buffers — see the power-tap warning above.
KEY FINDING — the active electrode and the RLD are not the same thing

The active electrode fixes per-electrode source impedance (a buffer on each signal electrode). The RLD/BIAS fixes whole-body common-mode (one feedback electrode driving the body). They solve different problems and you generally use both: buffers on the signal electrodes, RLD on a body-reference electrode.

SETTLING — a real interaction

The ADS1299 input is a switched-capacitor front end with a ~20 pF input capacitance behind a 3-MHz EMI filter. [20] Each sample yanks charge from the source. A raw MΩ electrode can't settle that in time; a buffer's few-ohm output can. So the buffer helps settling — but the op-amp must have enough bandwidth and low enough output impedance to absorb those switched-cap transients. Note the datasheet specifies only DC input impedance, not AC. [21]

06 Choosing the op-amp

What makes an op-amp a good active-electrode buffer

Three specs dominate: input bias current (low), input voltage noise (low, especially 1/f), and supply/EMI behavior. Real parts used in published active electrodes:

WHY THESE SPECS
  • Bias current — directly sets DC drift into the electrode (Section 04). Sub-pA is ideal.
  • 1/f (low-frequency) voltage noise — EEG lives at 0.1–40 Hz, right in the flicker-noise band; quote noise as µVpp over 0.1–10 Hz, not just nV/√Hz at 1 kHz.
  • EMI rejection — the buffer sits on a person acting as an antenna; parts with built-in RF rejection (MAX40023) keep phone/Wi-Fi from rectifying into a DC offset. [15]

Datasheet vs. paper: OPA2376's datasheet max bias current is 10 pA [4a]; the 0.2 pA figure is the typical value reported when it was used as an electrode buffer [4]. Design to the max, not the typ.

07 Putting it together

A working active-electrode recipe for the ADS1299

If you already have the ADS1299 running, here's the minimum you add per electrode.

×1
Op-amp follower on each signal electrode (OPA2376 / OPA378-class)
MΩ–GΩ
Rbias from + input to reference — DC path for Ib
Guard
Output drives cable shield / guard ring
±2.5 V
Power down the cable, ideally off DVDD/GND not AVDD/AVSS
INxP
Buffered output → channel input; reference via SRB1
RLD
Keep the BIAS/right-leg drive on a body electrode — still needed

Reported results from ADS1299 + active-electrode designs: input impedance up to 3.5 GΩ and CMRR of 139 dB in one design [6a]; input-referred noise around 4.5 µVrms (0.5–200 Hz). [6a]

SANITY CHECK BEFORE YOU BUILD

If you're using wet Ag/AgCl electrodes on short cables, try the ADS1299 without active electrodes first — it already rivals lab EEG below 40 Hz. [8] Add active electrodes when you move to dry electrodes, long cables, or a moving subject, which is where the buffer earns its cost.
