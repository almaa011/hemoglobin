ADS1292R vs AD8232

A datasheet-grounded comparison of the TI ADS1292R (24-bit dual-channel ADC + AFE) against the ADI AD8232 (analog-only single-lead front-end), using the AD8232 dev-board page on this site as the baseline. Covers architecture, noise/CMRR, features, known pitfalls, power, design complexity, and cost — with every claim traced to a datasheet, TI application note, or verified pricing/status page.

2026-07-07

01 Bottom line

Which one, and when

For a serious battery-powered ECG device — one that needs more than a single heart-rate trace — the ADS1292R is the better silicon. It replaces the AD8232's fixed-gain analog chain plus your MCU's onboard ADC with a purpose-built 24-bit front-end that has 40 dB more CMRR headroom, real lead-off detection with selectable current levels, an integrated right-leg-drive amplifier, and a respiration-impedance channel the AD8232 simply does not have [1]. The AD8232 remains the better choice when the whole point is "wire an analog voltage to whatever ADC I already have" — it needs no SPI stack, no register programming, and costs a fraction as much in bare-die pricing [9][10].

KEY FINDING

The architectural difference is not cosmetic: the AD8232 datasheet block diagram ends at an analog OUTPUT pin feeding your MCU's ADC [AD1]; the ADS1292R datasheet block diagram ends at an SPI bus with a 24-bit digital word already on it [1]. Every downstream design decision — sample timing, noise floor, filter design, firmware complexity — follows from that one fork in the signal chain.

CONFLICT — is the ADS1292R actually a good design choice?

A 2022 peer-reviewed survey of bioamplifier ICs states flatly that "the development of portable AFE-based cardiographs on chips from Texas Instruments with fewer channels, such as ADS1292R (two-channel system) and ADS1294R (four-channel system), was particularly unsuccessful" [11] — without explaining why (the paper gives no cost, technical, or adoption reasoning). This sits awkwardly against directly-verified facts: the ADS1292R is TI's ACTIVE production status (not NRND, not EOL) [7], it is in Digi-Key stock today [9], and TI shipped a 2019 reference design (TIDA-01614) built around it for a multiparameter patient monitor [8]. This page does not resolve that conflict — it is surfaced here as-is so you can weigh a strongly-worded but unexplained academic claim against concrete production/design-win evidence.

02 Architecture

Analog front-end vs. front-end-plus-ADC

The AD8232 is exactly what its datasheet title says: a "single-lead heart rate monitor front end" — an instrumentation amp (fixed ×100 gain), a 2-pole high-pass filter, a 3-pole low-pass/gain stage, and a right-leg-drive amplifier, all analog, ending in a single-ended voltage you feed into whatever ADC your MCU has [AD1] (see the companion AD8232 dev boards page for the full breakdown). The ADS1292R integrates the equivalent front-end and the data converter: two independent PGAs (gains 1/2/3/4/6/8/12, not a fixed ×100) each feeding its own 24-bit delta-sigma ADC, with a shared SPI digital interface delivering finished samples — no external ADC, no MCU ADC channel consumed [1].

Signal chain: AD8232 (top) vs ADS1292R (bottom) datasheet block diagrams, simplified

The AD8232 stops at an analog pin; every downstream stage (sampling, quantization, digital filtering) is built by you. The ADS1292R's PGA→ADC→SPI chain plus its RLD/lead-off/respiration blocks are all on one die [1][AD1].

What this means for your design

ADS1292R implications

  • Frees your MCU's ADC entirely — SPI is the only interface [1]
  • Two independently programmable-gain, simultaneously-sampled channels — true 2-lead ECG without external muxing [1]
  • 24-bit resolution means the gain/quantization tradeoff your MCU's 10-12 bit ADC forces on the AD8232 mostly disappears
  • Requires firmware: SPI driver, register configuration, DRDY-synchronized reads [1]

AD8232 implications

  • Any MCU with a single analog input pin works — no digital protocol at all
  • Fixed ×100 IA gain, external RC sets the rest of the band — simple but inflexible [AD1] (see baseline page, §02)
  • Single channel only — a second lead needs a second AD8232
  • Debug with a multimeter/scope on one pin; no register map to get wrong

THE CMRR GAP IS THE HEADLINE NUMBER

80 dB vs 105–120 dB is not a marginal improvement — that's a 25–40 dB gap, or roughly 18–100x better rejection of common-mode mains hum before any RLD help. The AD8232's 80 dB is a big reason that page's known-issues section is full of "mains hum" complaints [AD-hum] that require external shielding, battery power, and digital filtering to work around. The ADS1292R still needs a driven-reference electrode for a clean trace, but it starts from a much stronger position.

One caution worth flagging: the datasheet's own THD test footnote for "ADS1292R channel 1" specifies a 47 nF PGA filter capacitor, while the PGA Settings section text explicitly recommends 4.7 nF as the typical/general value and states the respiration channel should use 4.7 nF [1]. That's a 10x discrepancy between two places in the same document. This was not independently confirmed as a TI-acknowledged typo — treat it as an internal datasheet inconsistency to verify against your own board bring-up, and default to the 4.7 nF recommendation in the PGA Settings prose unless you're specifically replicating the THD test condition.

04 Features

Lead-off, RLD, respiration, power modes

Lead-off detection

The AD8232 offers AC or DC lead-off detection with two comparator outputs, selected by one pin [AD1]. The ADS1292R's lead-off block is more configurable: four selectable excitation current levels (6 nA, 22 nA, 6 µA, 22 µA) with ±10% current accuracy and ±10 mV comparator threshold accuracy, all register-set rather than pin-strapped [1]. More current settings means you can trade detection speed against injected-current safety margin per application, something the AD8232's fixed excitation can't do.

Right-leg drive (RLD)

Both parts have an RLD amplifier for active common-mode cancellation. The mechanism is the same idea (invert the sensed common-mode voltage and drive it back through a reference electrode), but the ADS1292R's is a closed-loop system you can tune: TI's dedicated app note on the shared ADS129x RLD architecture shows CMR improving from roughly 70–83 dB with no RLD feedback to 93–96 dB with the loop closed, using RF/CF component choices to set closed-loop bandwidth (~106 Hz in the tested example) [5]. That gain comes with a real stability hazard: the app note is explicit that the protection resistor and cable/electrode impedance form a low-pass filter whose pole can be as small as 2 kHz, and that if an RLD-driven electrode (RA/LA) loses contact, the loop can drive the patient's common-mode voltage into a rail and stall the PGA — TI's guidance is to open the RLD loop whenever a lead-off condition is detected [5].

DESIGN PITFALL — RLD + lead-off is not "set and forget"

Closing the RLD feedback loop and running lead-off detection are not independent decisions. If you leave RLD closed while an electrode is disconnected, the datasheet's own app note warns the loop can rail the front-end and stop it working [5]. Your firmware needs to watch the lead-off status bits and open the RLD loop (or fall back to a fixed reference) the moment a lead-off event fires. This is a genuine extra state machine the AD8232 — which has no closed feedback loop to manage — does not require.

Respiration impedance (ADS1292R only, no AD8232 equivalent)

This is the ADS1292R's standout feature. It injects a small AC excitation current (32 or 64 kHz internally, or an external 32–64 kHz clock) through the electrodes and demodulates the resulting amplitude change to track thoracic impedance, which correlates with breathing [1][6]. TI's method note describes the physics directly: typical baseline thoracic impedance is ~500 Ω, with the actual respiratory signal (ΔR) being only 0.1–1 Ω on top of that — a small AC signal riding a much larger DC-ish baseline [6]. The ADS1292R's own datasheet specifies impedance range 2,000–10,000 Ω, impedance measurement noise of 40 mΩPP, and register-selectable demodulation phase (0°, 112.5°, 168.75°) [1].

DESIGN PITFALL — respiration accuracy is phase-sensitive

TI's own respiration app note shows the demodulator's output gain is proportional to the cosine of the phase difference between the modulation and demodulation clocks, falling to zero gain at a 90° mismatch [6]. The ADS1292R gives you a fixed set of phase-shift register options (0/112.5/168.75°) rather than continuous calibration, so getting a clean respiration signal depends on your electrode/cable impedance landing close to one of those fixed points. This is a real accuracy ceiling on the respiration channel, not a bug — budget bench time to characterize it against your actual electrode/cable combination rather than trusting the datasheet numbers to hold as-is on your hardware.

Power modes

The ADS1292R has three explicit states in the datasheet: normal, standby, and power-down, plus documented wake-up timing — power-on-reset to first valid sample (DRDY low) takes 32 ms, while standby-to-DRDY or power-down-to-DRDY takes 10 ms [1]. The AD8232 has a simpler binary shutdown pin (SDN) with sub-200 nA shutdown current and no register-based standby state (see baseline page, Core electrical specs table) [AD1].

05 Known issues & errata

What actually bites people

NO DEDICATED ERRATA SHEET FOUND

Unlike some TI product lines, no standalone public errata document for the ADS1291/ADS1292/ADS1292R family was located after two independent search passes plus a direct check of the TI product page. TI appears to manage device-specific issues through its E2E support forum rather than a published errata sheet for this part. This is reported as a finding, not a gap — if TI publishes one in the future, it isn't indexed anywhere this research could reach.

Two forum-sourced claims researched for this page — a reported SPI RDATAC/DRDY timing gotcha and a reset/clock-pin pull-up question — could not be independently verified (the TI E2E forum returned HTTP 403 to every automated fetch attempt in this session) and are deliberately omitted rather than presented as fact. If you hit SPI communication issues, TI's own guidance elsewhere confirms the digital interface uses SPI Mode 1 (CPOL=0, CPHA=1) — that's directly datasheet-verified [1] — so start there before assuming a timing-margin bug.

06 Design complexity & cost

What it costs you in BOM, layout, and firmware

ADS1292R

  • Two power domains: AVDD (2.7–5.25V) and DVDD (1.7–3.6V), separately supplied [1]
  • SPI Mode 1 digital interface (CPOL=0, CPHA=1) — needs an MCU with SPI and firmware to configure the register map [1]
  • Thermal pad on the VQFN package must be connected to AVSS — adds a layout constraint [1]
  • 32-pin TQFP (5×5mm) or VQFN (4×4mm) — real-world open-source shield needs an onboard 3.3V regulator plus logic-level translators to bridge a 5V Arduino [10]
  • TI's own reference design (TIDA-01614) runs it on a 1-cell 3.7V Li-ion battery for a full multiparameter patient monitor with ECG at 500 SPS / 100 µV sensitivity [8]

AD8232

  • Single supply, 2.0–3.5V — one rail, no sequencing to think about [AD1]
  • Analog output pin only — any MCU with an ADC input works, no digital protocol
  • Breadboard-friendly: SparkFun's SEN-12650 breakout is a 9-pin part with a 3.5mm electrode jack [AD1]
  • Filter band is set by external RC on the board, not registers — changing it means changing components, not firmware

KEY FINDING — status check

The ADS1292R shows ACTIVE product lifecycle status directly on ti.com — not NRND (Not Recommended for New Designs), not EOL [7]. Combined with current Digi-Key stock and TI's own 2019 reference design built around it, there's no production-status reason to avoid it for a new design as of this research session.

07 Recommendation

Putting it together

Choose the ADS1292R if your device needs any of: more than one ECG lead, respiration monitoring, diagnostic-grade CMRR headroom, or you'd rather spend firmware effort than fight an 80 dB CMRR ceiling and a fixed analog filter band. Budget real engineering time for the RLD-loop/lead-off interaction and for characterizing respiration phase accuracy on your specific electrode/cable combination — those are genuine, datasheet-and-appnote-documented design tasks, not hypothetical risks [5][6].

Choose the AD8232 if you want the fastest path to a single-lead heart-rate trace, you already have a spare ADC channel and don't want to own an SPI/register-configuration stack, or the extra ~$7–13/unit (bare-IC pricing at volume) matters more than the extra 25–40 dB of CMRR and the missing respiration channel [9][10].

Neither part is a medical device on its own, and nothing on this page should be read as clearance for diagnostic or patient-safety-critical use — TI's own ADS1292R evaluation kit documentation states explicitly it is "not for diagnostic use... not for use with a defibrillator" [SLAU384], and the same caution applies a fortiori to a bring-up board built around the bare IC.
