Motion Artifact Rejection in PPG — An Engineer's Guide

Every time a wearer walks, taps, or flexes their wrist, the optical pulse sensor picks up noise that can dwarf the actual heartbeat. This page covers the physics of why that happens and the hardware + algorithm toolkit used to fight it — from datasheet-level AFE tricks to the adaptive filters running on the wrist.

2026-07-09

↓ start reading
TL;DR

Motion artifact (MA) isn't one problem — it's two. Sensor displacement shifts the optical path length ("baseline drift"), while tissue squeezing under the sensor changes the light transmission itself ("time-varying system parameters," TVSP). Neither is removable with a simple fixed filter because their frequency content overlaps the heartbeat's [3].

Real numbers: unfiltered PPG beat-detection sensitivity falls from 99% while walking to 82.49% while running [12], and hand motions like rubbing can push SpO2 error past 10% RMSE [9].

The fix is two-layered: AFE hardware does ambient-light and dark-current subtraction (~60 dB rejection) [10] and fast LED pulsing to cut 1/f noise, while firmware runs adaptive filters (LMS/RLS/ANC) that use a 3-axis accelerometer as a "what the noise looks like" reference to subtract the correlated motion component out of the optical signal.

Done well, this combination is dramatic: one validated method cut heart-rate error from 11.47 BPM to 0.92 BPM during hard running — a ~92% reduction [16]. Done poorly (or absent), consumer wearables still show large, sport-dependent errors, and several algorithm classes have documented failure modes this page covers explicitly.

01 The physics

What a "motion artifact" actually is

A PPG sensor is an optical bridge measuring blood volume — anything that changes the light path other than blood volume is noise. The sensor shines light (usually green, red, or infrared) into tissue and measures how much comes back; blood volume changes with each heartbeat modulate that return signal by a few percent [3].

Motion corrupts this in two physically distinct ways, and conflating them is the single biggest reason naive filtering fails.

Two failure mechanisms, not one
source-verified, PMC12473256
MechanismWhat physically happensCircuit analogy
Baseline driftSensor shifts relative to the artery — the optical path length itself changesLike your photodiode slowly rotating away from a fixed light source — a slow DC offset wander
Time-varying system parameters (TVSP)Tissue compresses/deforms under the sensor, changing how light transmits through it — independent of any blood-volume changeLike the gain of your transimpedance amplifier randomly modulating mid-measurement — a multiplicative distortion riding on every harmonic of the real signal

TVSP is the harder of the two: it's quantified at roughly 1% heart-rate variation and <5% pulse-amplitude change per event, and it cannot be removed by ordinary filtering, empirical mode decomposition, or compressed sensing [3] — the distortion sits on top of the same frequencies as the real pulse.

Fixed-frequency filters fail because motion and heartbeat overlap in frequency. A resting heart rate and a walking cadence can both land in the 1–3 Hz band, so a low-pass or band-pass filter tuned to "heart rate range" cannot tell them apart [4].

02 How bad it gets

The scale of the problem, in numbers

Motion artifact amplitude routinely exceeds the pulsatile signal it's corrupting. In wrist-worn devices specifically, MA "can have much larger amplitude than the pulsatile component in the PPG," and wrist sensors suffer worse than finger-clip sensors because the wrist itself flexes [2].

Warning — even "clean" data isn't clean

Even everyday hand motions most people wouldn't call "exercise" — tapping, rubbing, turning a book page, using a tablet — pushed SpO2 RMSE above 4% on at least one tested consumer pulse oximeter [9]. If you're designing for daily-life use, "resting" is not a safe assumption.

03 Hardware-level defenses

What the AFE does before any algorithm runs

The first line of defense is entirely analog — reject ambient light and dark current before the ADC ever sees them. This doesn't remove motion artifact directly, but it stops motion-modulated ambient light (sunlight flicker, indoor lighting) from adding a second noise source on top of it.

Synchronous detection signal chain
ADI ADPD4100 architecture

Each measurement cycle takes a dark reading (LEDs off), a red reading, an IR reading, and an ambient-only reading, then subtracts dark + ambient from the LED-on readings before conversion [10]. This is done every cycle, at LED pulse widths as short as 1 µs, specifically to fight 1/f noise [10].

Wavelength choice is itself a motion-robustness decision

Green light is absorbed roughly 10–14x more strongly in the epidermis than in deeper skin layers — measured absorption coefficients at 532 nm are 1.44 mm⁻¹ (epidermis) vs. 0.10 mm⁻¹ (dermis) vs. 0.15 mm⁻¹ (subcutaneous fat) [17]. Longer wavelengths (595–800 nm, i.e. red/near-IR) reach deeper into the dermis, and 980–1064 nm reaches the subcutaneous fat layer [17].

That's why fitness-tracker HR sensors are almost always green while clinical SpO2 needs red+IR: green stays shallow (good SNR near the surface, more resistant to deep-tissue motion noise), but SpO2 math needs the red/IR absorption-ratio physics regardless of penetration depth [7]. This is a physical tradeoff, not just a marketing choice — a deeper-penetrating wavelength picks up more of the tissue-deformation (TVSP) component described in Section 1.

04 Firmware-level defenses

Adaptive filtering: teaching the system what "motion" looks like

The core idea: feed the filter a reference signal that contains the noise but not the heartbeat, and let it subtract the correlated part out. A 3-axis accelerometer is the standard reference — it can't see the pulse (a pulse's acceleration signature is far below what even a sensitive accelerometer resolves [3]), but it does see the body motion causing the artifact.

Key finding — don't conflate two different accelerometer roles

Some sources point out accelerometers "can't measure the pulse" [3] — true, but that's not their job here. Their job is supplying a correlated noise reference for adaptive filtering, not sensing the heartbeat directly. Keep these separate: "accelerometer can't see the pulse" and "accelerometer is useless for motion rejection" are not the same claim.

The standard architecture

  • Sampling: Maxim's reference spec runs both PPG and the accelerometer at 25 Hz (38–43 ms interval), synchronized to within 25 ms [13].
  • Activity classification: the accelerometer stream is independently classified into rest/walk/run/bike/other states, without needing the PPG signal at all [13].
  • Gating: a reliable SpO2 reading requires >30 s of continuous signal with "Low PI," "Low SNR," and "Motion" flags all clear [14] — i.e., the system is designed to refuse an answer rather than report a bad one.
  • Filter classes in use: LMS-family adaptive filters show up twice in verified sources — constrained ICA + LMS beats plain FFT-LMS and moving-average filtering [4], and an LMS-Newton variant drives the accelerometer-referenced ANC result below [16]. RLS is frequently cited alongside LMS in the broader literature, but no verified source in this research pass confirmed a specific RLS-for-PPG result, so it's omitted here. Band-pass filtering alone leaves 11.47 BPM average error. Adding cascaded ANC with a 3-axis accelerometer reference drops that to 1.59 BPM; the full method (ANC + frequency tracking + notch filtering) reaches 0.92 BPM — about a 92% reduction from the PPG-only baseline [16].

Alternative reference: use a second optical channel instead of an accelerometer

One study found that a wrist accelerometer records sensor/limb displacement but structurally cannot record internal tissue deformation, since that deformation doesn't move the wrist itself [15]. Their proposed fix: use a second PPG wavelength channel as the noise reference instead, since it's affected similarly by the same tissue-level motion [15].

05 Where it still breaks

Known failure modes and unresolved disagreements

No single rejection method is complete — every approach here has a documented blind spot. This section covers what the "adversarial" research angle surfaced: places where the clean story from Sections 3–4 doesn't hold up.

Conflict — "best algorithm" is contested, not settled

One study found a Kalman-smoother approach outperforming NLMS/RLS/conventional Kalman filtering for artifact removal — but that specific result is for near-infrared spectroscopy signal denoising, not PPG, and it explicitly avoided a backward/fixed-interval smoother as impractical for real-time use [18]. Meanwhile, a separate 2026 study found a spectral-subtraction method (SPECMAR) as the worst performer under vigorous motion (4.80 BPM max error, highest of all tested algorithms) and found finite-state-machine-based frameworks limited by "considering fewer parameters when judging signal states" [20]. No source in this research directly compares Kalman vs. wavelet vs. ICA vs. ML head-to-head under identical motion conditions — treat any single "best algorithm" claim you encounter elsewhere with caution.

  • Accelerometer blind spot — fine motion, still wrist. Finger tapping can leave a wrist-worn accelerometer showing no motion at all while the PPG signal is heavily distorted, because the wrist itself never moves [2].
  • Sensitivity collapses with motion intensity. Beat-detection sensitivity drops from 99% (walking) to 82.49% (running) in one study, and published test protocols themselves are typically limited to walking/fast-walking/running — real-world motion diversity (sports, daily activities) is under-tested in the literature [12].
  • ML generalization risk. Over half of reviewed machine-learning studies for PPG artifact detection don't show evidence of testing on genuinely unseen data, and models trained on finger PPG risk failing at wrist/forehead/earlobe sites due to differing signal morphology [19].
  • Proprietary black boxes. Consumer-wearable biometric algorithms are generally not open to public scrutiny [21], and at least one major vendor (Fitbit) does not grant researchers access to raw PPG/accelerometer signals — only its own processed output — making it impossible for outside researchers to isolate whether an error originates in hardware, firmware, or algorithm [22].
  • Cross-brand variability is large. In one review, no wearable brand stayed within ±3% energy-expenditure error more than 13% of the time [21] — a proxy for how unevenly motion-compensation quality is implemented across the market.
A secondary (non-primary) source reporting on real-world Fitbit heart-rate accuracy claims bias reaching about −16.5 BPM in racket/team sports like badminton and soccer, versus near-zero bias for steady running. 06 Regulatory context

What standards actually require

Regulators mandate motion testing but leave the numeric pass/fail bar mostly to the standard itself, which is paywalled. The FDA's pulse-oximeter 510(k) guidance requires manufacturers to test and describe motion characteristics (amplitude, type, frequency) per ISO 80601-2-61:2011, Clause 201.12.1.102 [25].
